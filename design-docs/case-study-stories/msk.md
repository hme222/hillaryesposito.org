## Q1: What was the problem, in the terms it was actually described to you at the time?

Across three different roles at MSKCC, found myself using Lean Six Sigma to
change methodology and remove the "fat" of systems/processes — aligned with
UX before she had a name for it as UX.

**Chapter 1 — Office Coordinator: the send-to-EMR button.**
Noticed a lot of the work was printing messaging from the physician/clinical
team and patient that already existed on the system, then waiting for
filing/processing to get it back into the system anyway. Explored different
departments to find a workflow that was simpler, more efficient, and still
abided by institutional standards. Worked with IT and UX teams to suggest a
"send to EMR" button to save time and physical printing.

**Chapter 2 — Administrative Assistant: the CPR certification deadline.**
Worked with a project manager to collect CPR certifications. MDs were slow
to submit. The team's default assumption-driven fix was to extend the
deadline by 4 months. Worked directly with physicians to change the
language used in the CPR collection process — making it more transparent
and aligned with legal — then had the developer team and UX team implement
the changes. Result: the entire department submitted a month *before* the
original deadline. No extension needed.

**Chapter 3 — Trainer/Facilitator: adapting teaching to the room.**
Used research and insights to adjust teaching methods per class. Worked
with the design team to change lessons to fit the actual audience —
different treatment for managers vs. senior leaders.

## Q2: What was your specific contribution, separate from the team?

**Chapter 1:** Found the pattern herself. Reached out to each department to
navigate the realities and cons of a potential change and how to work
around them. Packaged and presented the idea at MSKCC's yearly Process
Improvement Fair. IT/UX built the button after that — her ownership is the
diagnosis, the cross-department feasibility check, and the internal pitch,
not the implementation.

**Chapter 2:** Worked with the UX team to speak on behalf of the MDs, based
on direct conversations with them, so the language change was grounded in
what physicians actually needed rather than the team's assumptions.

**Chapter 3:** Gathered and explained how the lessons were landing — how
they came across, how classes were receiving them, how the room felt
around how lessons were built — and brought that diagnostic to the design
team, who then adjusted the lessons.

**Throughline across all three (named back to her, confirmed by the shape
of every answer):** in each chapter, the contribution is the same *shape* —
go find out what's actually happening from the people living it, then
translate that into a diagnosis a technical/design team can act on. Not
three unrelated stories; one skill applied three times.

## Q3: Decision/trade-off made that someone else might have made differently

**Chapter 1:** Not a trade-off so much as an honest limitation worth
keeping as-is: the idea was deemed low priority by IT (easy fix, but a lot
of "work" relative to its size on their plate). She had to keep bringing it
up repeatedly to get it prioritized.

**Chapter 2:** Chose "fix the language and process" over the team's default
"extend the deadline by 4 months." Result: department hit the *original*
deadline a month early.

**Chapter 3:** Introduced a 5-minute trivia warm-up before each session
(topics varied) to shift the room into a learning mindset before content
started. Classes responded well to it.

## Q4: What would you do differently?

Across all three: would implement more UX thinking and research into each
project. (Not chapter-specific — a general retrospective answer.)
