# Neurodiversity Review Source Framework

Use this reference to ground the review without turning a resource list into a universal checklist.

## Primary baseline

W3C, [Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/), Working Group Note:

- help users understand controls and purpose;
- help users find what they need;
- use clear content;
- prevent and support correction of mistakes;
- help users focus;
- avoid relying on memory;
- provide help and support;
- support adaptation and personalization;
- include people with cognitive and learning disabilities in research, design, and testing.

The W3C document is supplemental guidance beyond WCAG. Do not present it as a WCAG conformance requirement.

## Practitioner synthesis

Stéphanie Walter, [Neurodiversity and UX: Essential Resources for Cognitive Accessibility](https://stephaniewalter.design/blog/neurodiversity-and-ux-essential-resources-for-cognitive-accessibility/), published April 25, 2024 and updated April 2026.

Walter's resource map broadens the review beyond a generic cognitive-load checklist. It points to:

- dyslexia and literacy: text presentation, typography, reading effort, and clear language;
- dyscalculia and low numeracy: alternatives for numerical concepts, quantities, units, charts, and calculations;
- autism: predictable interaction, literal communication, sensory control, and inclusive research practice;
- ADHD: clear structure, focus protection, task resumption, flexible timing, positive feedback, and reduced cognitive stress;
- neuroinclusive workplaces and education: environments, collaboration, research accommodations, and organizational practices.

Treat these as areas to investigate, not condition-based prescriptions. A pattern that helps one person may be neutral or harmful to another.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, in order:

1. observed evidence from the current product and participant research;
2. applicable standards and official public guidance;
3. peer-reviewed or clearly scoped research;
4. practitioner synthesis;
5. a clearly labelled hypothesis to validate.

## Language

Use identity-first or person-first language according to the individual or community's stated preference. When no preference is known, use neutral constructions such as “neurodivergent people,” “people with cognitive and learning disabilities,” or name the access need directly.

Avoid:

- “the neurodiverse” as a noun;
- “suffers from,” “high/low functioning,” or deficit-only framing;
- claiming a design is “ADHD-friendly” or “autism-friendly” without evidence;
- presenting one neurotype as a monolith.

## Validation rule

The reviewer identifies likely barriers; neurodivergent participants determine whether the experience works for them. For high-consequence products or condition-specific claims, usability testing or co-design is required before sign-off.
